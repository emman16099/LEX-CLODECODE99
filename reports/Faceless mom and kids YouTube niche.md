# Parents pay while kids only watch

The idea is half right. A faceless, long-form YouTube channel can make money in the "mom and kids" space, but only if the videos are made **for parents**. Videos made **for little kids** pay the least of any type of video on YouTube. A study of 300 paid channels found that kids videos earn a middle value of just **$0.33 for every 1,000 views**, while lifestyle videos earn **$2.98** ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/youtube-monetization-statistics-2026)). Kids channels also face strict child-privacy rules and a big crackdown on cheap AI cartoons, and they compete with giant studios that started 10 to 20 years ago. Parent videos earn roughly **5 to 10 times more per view**, can use brand deals, and fit how parents use YouTube: to search for answers. Our verdict is that faceless kids content is **a poor bet for a newcomer**, and faceless parenting content is **a fair bet with a modest ceiling**. Either way, most new channels never earn real money. A newcomer should plan for a slow build over years.

## Two different channels hide inside one idea

"Mom and kids" can mean two very different things. The first is a channel that **kids watch**, such as nursery songs or cartoons. The second is a channel that **moms and dads watch**, such as sleep tips, pregnancy week by week, or tantrum advice. YouTube treats these two kinds very differently, so the money is very different too.

To follow the money, you need four terms. **CPM** means "cost per mille." It is what advertisers pay for 1,000 ad views. **RPM** means "revenue per mille." It is what the creator actually keeps for every 1,000 views of a video, after YouTube takes its share. **YPP** is the YouTube Partner Program. A channel must join it to earn ad money, and it needs **1,000 subscribers and 4,000 watch hours in the past 12 months** to get in ([lenspov](https://lenspov.com/research/youtube-cpm-by-niche-2026/)). **COPPA** is a US law that protects the privacy of children under 13. It changed kids YouTube for good, as the next section shows.

| | Made **for kids** | Made **for parents** |
|---|---|---|
| Typical RPM | **$0.33** middle value; about $0.16 to $2 range ([AIR](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/youtube-monetization-statistics-2026); [TrendReport](https://trendreport.com/intelligence/youtube-rpm-by-niche/)) | About **$2 to $8** for US-heavy audiences ([tubetools](https://tubetools.online/en/youtube-cpm-by-niche.html); [TrendReport](https://trendreport.com/intelligence/youtube-rpm-by-niche/)) |
| Ad money from 1 million views (estimate) | About **$300 to $2,000** | About **$2,000 to $8,000** |
| Comments, bell alerts, end screens | Turned off | Allowed |
| Brand deals | Hard and limited | Common |
| Biggest risk | AI-slop crackdown, low pay, giant rivals | Trust, since parents follow real people |
| Verdict | **Poor bet for a newcomer** | **Fair bet with a modest ceiling** |

## Kids videos lost most of their ad money in 2020

In 2019, Google and YouTube paid a record **$170 million** fine. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) said YouTube had collected data from children to target ads without asking parents ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/09/google-youtube-will-pay-record-170-million-alleged-violations-childrens-privacy-law)). Since January 2020, every creator must mark videos as "made for kids" if children are the main audience. Those videos can only show plain ads that match the video topic. They cannot show "personalized" ads that follow a viewer's interests, and those personalized ads pay much more. Made-for-kids videos also lose comments, the notification bell, end screens, memberships, and the "save to playlist" button ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9632097?hl=en)). The creator is legally responsible for marking videos correctly, so a channel cannot dodge the rule ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9527654)).

The hit was large. A co-founder of the kids studio Animaj said kids creators lost **about 50% of their YouTube earnings on average** ([Kidscreen](https://kidscreen.com/2025/10/27/how-kids-creators-are-making-youtube-work-again/)). An academic paper hosted by the FTC reports creator drops of **60% to 90%**, and one kids creator saw ad prices fall **73%** ([Johnson et al., FTC PDF](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/COPPAcalypse-the-YouTube-settlements-impact-on-kids-content-garrett-a.-johnson.pdf)). Today kids videos sit at the very bottom of the pay table. Their middle RPM of $0.33 is about **31 times lower** than education and science videos at $10.22 ([AIR Media-Tech](https://air.io/en/air-data-findings/which-youtube-niche-makes-the-most-money-in-2026-ranked-by-real-rpm-and-cpm)). In plain terms, a kids channel with 1 million views a month might earn only a few hundred dollars from ads.

## The kids giants won early and earn off YouTube

The famous kids channels make the idea look easy, but their history tells a harder story. **CoComelon** started in 2006 and only broke out after a 2018 rebrand, about **12 years** later ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoComelon)). **Super Simple Songs** also started in 2006 and took about **12 years** to reach 10 million subscribers ([C21 Media](https://www.c21media.net/department/thought-leadership/skyship-keeps-it-super-simple/)). Both grew before the market was crowded and before the 2020 kids ad rules. CoComelon had about **20 staff** when it was sold ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/cocomelon-youtube-disney-netflix-dreamworks-children-media/)). These are studios with full teams.

The real money for these winners comes from outside YouTube ads. Moonbug, which owns CoComelon, Blippi and Little Baby Bum, was bought for about **$3 billion** in 2021 ([Variety](https://variety.com/2021/biz/news/moonbug-acquisition-disney-tom-staggs-kevin-mayer-1235124553)). CoComelon moves to Disney+ in 2027 in a deal reported at "tens of millions of dollars" a year ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/05/27/cocomelon-rights-move-netflix-to-disney-plus/)). Pinkfong, the company behind "Baby Shark," made **97.4 billion won** in 2024 and joined the Korean stock market in November 2025 ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2025/11/18/baby-shark-pinkfong-stock-market-ipo-south-korea/)). Toys, TV deals and tours pay the bills. Low YouTube RPMs only work for them because they get billions of views. CoComelon alone pulled about **3.5 to 4 billion views a month** ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/cocomelon-youtube-disney-netflix-dreamworks-children-media/)).

One more lesson stands out. The fastest new kids star is **Ms Rachel**, who started in 2019 and grew to about **13 million subscribers by January 2025** ([Yahoo/Business Insider](https://www.yahoo.com/entertainment/youtube-star-ms-rachel-built-143645823.html)). She shows her face. Every faceless kids winner above either started before 2012 or is backed by a TV show, like Peppa Pig or Bluey. We found no recent solo faceless kids channel that grew to that size the honest way.

Demand is still strong. YouTube's share of US TV-screen viewing reached about **13.8% in June 2026** ([voxbooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/youtube-tv-screen-viewing-share-statistics-2026/)), and parents often leave long kids playlists running on the living-room TV. The problem is who gets those views. Big studios with 10 or more years of old videos soak up most of them.

## The AI cartoon boom brought a crackdown

Many "faceless kids channel" courses now push AI-made songs and cartoons. This is exactly the content YouTube and the press are fighting. In July 2025, YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" rule to **"inauthentic content."** It says mass-produced, template-like videos with little change between them cannot earn money, no matter what tool made them ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/09/youtube-prepares-crackdown-on-mass-produced-and-repetitive-videos-as-concern-over-ai-slop-grows)). In February 2026, the New York Times reviewed more than **1,000 videos** shown to young children. It found AI cartoons with warped faces and wrong facts, often sold as "educational." YouTube then removed all five channels the Times named from YPP, which also cut them off from the YouTube Kids app ([NYT](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/26/us/ai-videos-children-youtube.html); [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/social-media/investigation-finds-youtube-is-serving-mindless-ai-slop-to-toddlers-and-preschoolers/)). After a second report, YouTube shut down **six more channels** for breaking its rules and one for spam ([The 74](https://www.the74million.org/zero2eight/ai-slop-is-flooding-childrens-media-parents-should-be-very-alarmed/); [Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/youtube/youtube-strikes-down-channels-after-investigation-into-bizarre-ai-content-for-kids-3326231/)).

The pressure keeps growing. In April 2026, more than **135 groups and 102 experts** asked YouTube to ban AI content from YouTube Kids and ban AI "made for kids" videos on main YouTube ([Fairplay](https://fairplayforkids.org/youtube-stop-ai-slop-for-kids-says-letter-from-fairplay-over-200-experts-including-jonathan-haidt/)). YouTube says AI content in the Kids app is already limited to "a small set of high-quality channels" ([Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2026/04/01/ai-slop-200-organizations-letter-youtube-google)). This has happened before. In 2017, during the "Elsagate" scandal, YouTube pulled ads from nearly **2 million videos** and more than **50,000 channels** that posed as family-friendly ([Vice News](https://news.vice.com/story/youtube-kills-ads-on-50000-channels-as-advertisers-flee-over-disturbing-child-content)). The pattern repeats: a flood of cheap videos, then news stories, then a purge.

To be fair, some AI channels do earn big. Kapwing found 278 top channels made only of AI "slop," with an estimated **$117 million a year** in total revenue ([Kapwing](https://www.kapwing.com/resources/ai-slop/)). One Bloomberg source said AI kids songs can earn "hundreds of dollars a day," but she also sells lessons on how to do it ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-12-03/ai-slop-youtube-videos-for-kids-pretend-to-be-educational)). These are the lucky few at the top, and many of them face the same purge risk. We found no checked case study of a small faceless kids channel with real, shared earnings.

**Verdict for content made FOR kids:** the hypothesis does not hold for a newcomer. Pay is the lowest on YouTube, growth tools are switched off, the leaders are huge studios, and cheap AI output is under active attack. The only path that still works is a studio-style one: original characters, real effort, and money from toys, books or licensing later on.

## Parents pay better and search for help

Videos made for adults about parenting are a different story. They are not "made for kids," so they can show personalized ads. Estimates for parenting and family RPM run about **$2 to $6** ([tubetools](https://tubetools.online/en/youtube-cpm-by-niche.html)) or **$3 to $8** ([TrendReport](https://trendreport.com/intelligence/youtube-rpm-by-niche/)). These are blog estimates, not official numbers, and they assume most viewers live in the US. Family-money topics, like saving for college, can earn even more because finance ads pay well. Advertisers want parents. Baby and kids ad spending passed **$1.5 billion** from January to November 2023, up **20%** from the year before, and Huggies alone spent over **$108 million** ([MediaRadar](https://www.mediaradar.com/blog/blog/q4-2023-12-for-24-baby-kids-2)).

Brand deals add a second income. Parenting YouTubers are quoted at **$500 to $15,000 per sponsored video**, or about **$15 to $25 per 1,000 views** for a short sponsor mention ([LaunchPoint](https://www.launchpointhq.com/guides/rates/how-much-do-parenting-youtubers-charge)). That can be several times more than ad money. Amazon links pay less than people hope. Baby products earn just **3%**, so a $300 stroller earns about $9 ([Spreesy](https://spreesy.com/tools/amazon-influencer-earnings-calculator)).

Parents do use YouTube for help, though the best numbers are old. In 2016, **7 in 10 US moms** said they came to YouTube for parenting guidance ([Think with Google](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/1737/moms-on-youtube-online-video-trends.pdf)). Newer data adds a warning. In a 2025 survey, millennial moms named **Instagram (75.9%)** as the app that shapes their parenting views most, with YouTube at only **9.5%** ([Motherly](https://www.mother.ly/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/2025-Motherly-State-of-Motherhood-Survey-Results-1.pdf)). This suggests moms go to YouTube to **search for answers**, like "how to swaddle" or "pregnancy week 20," and follow personalities on Instagram. Search-style how-to videos suit a faceless channel well.

The catch is trust. The biggest parenting channels show a real person: Big Little Feelings has about **3.5 million** subscribers, and Emily Oster has about **590,000** ([Klint](https://tryklint.ai/influencers/parenting/)). When parents want screen-time advice, **61%** ask a doctor and **55%** ask other parents ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2020/07/28/parenting-children-in-the-age-of-screens/)). Faceless parenting channels that work tend to be brands or publishers. BabyCenter has about 705,000 subscribers but earns an estimated **$2,200 to $4,300 a month** from ads ([Nexlev](https://www.nexlev.io/youtube-stats/@babycenter); [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/@babycenter/)). The best proof that faceless can work is **Psych2Go**, an animated psychology channel with **13.2 million** subscribers and an estimated **$41,000 to $63,000 a month** in ad money ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/@psych2go/about/); [Nexlev](https://www.nexlev.io/youtube-stats/@psych2go/earnings)). Where the viewers live matters a lot too. FirstCry Parenting, aimed at India, has **1.66 million** subscribers but earns an estimated **under $1,000 a month** ([Nexlev](https://www.nexlev.io/youtube-stats/@firstcryparenting)).

**Verdict for content made FOR parents:** the hypothesis holds in part. The money per view is far better than kids content, and search demand is real. The ceiling is lower than for parenting creators who show their face, because trust and sponsor deals lean toward real people. Health and pregnancy topics also need careful, checked facts.

## Most new channels never get paid

Any honest answer must include the odds. A 2018 study found that the top **3% of channels** got about **85% of all views**. It also estimated that **96.5%** of creators earned less than the US poverty line from ads ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/40537244/96-5-of-youtube-creators-dont-make-above-the-u-s-poverty-line); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1354856517736979)). YouTube does not share what share of channels ever reach YPP. Most "I made $10,000 a month with a faceless channel" stories come from people who sell courses on how to do it. Treat them as ads.

So what should a realistic newcomer expect? Here is a rough guide built from the numbers above. These are our estimates, not promises. In year one, the most likely result is fewer than 1,000 subscribers and little or no income. A parenting channel that reaches **100,000 US-heavy views a month** might earn about **$200 to $800 a month** from ads, plus the odd sponsor. A kids channel with the same views might earn about **$30 to $200**. Reaching **1 million views a month** usually takes years of steady uploads, if it happens at all. The channels that last share a few habits: each video is clearly different and carefully made, a named narrator or brand voice gives the channel a personality, and health claims point to trusted sources like the American Academy of Pediatrics on screen. These habits also keep a channel on the right side of the "inauthentic content" rule.

Some things remain unknown. There is no official YouTube data on parenting RPM, no study comparing faceless and on-camera parenting channels, and no fresh data on how long moms watch long videos. The RPM ranges here come mostly from blogs and creator surveys.

## Conclusion

The phrase "mom and kids" joins two businesses that pull in opposite directions. Kids get the views, but parents bring the money. Kids attention is huge, yet law and policy have made it nearly worthless per view for anyone without a studio, a TV deal or a toy line. Parent attention is smaller, but advertisers pay to reach it, and it shows up through search, which a faceless channel can win. A newcomer who wants both can make parent-facing videos *about* kids, such as toddler sleep, milestones, or family budgeting, and stay out of the made-for-kids category completely.

The biggest shift in the last two years is about risk. In the kids space, "faceless" now often means "AI-made," and AI-made kids videos are what YouTube, the press and parent groups are trying to stamp out. The safe faceless channel of 2026 shows clear human work in every video. It earns trust through good sources and a steady voice in place of a face. And it plans to grow slowly, the way every lasting winner in this space has.
